\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Discrete approaches to quantum gravity, from Regge calculus
\citep{regge1961} to causal dynamical triangulations
\citep{ambjorn2012cdt}, replace smooth spacetime with combinatorial data:
simplices, gluings, and moves between configurations. A persistent conceptual
discomfort with such approaches is that geometric \emph{measure} --- edge
lengths, volumes, simplex counts --- appears to be minted from nothing as
configurations grow. In the sum-over-histories formulation this discomfort is
usually dissolved by fiat: histories of every size appear in the ensemble, and
a cosmological-constant term prices, but does not conserve, the total volume.

This paper takes the discomfort seriously and asks what a theory looks like if
one demands, from the start, that

\begin{enumerate}
  \item nothing is ever destroyed: evolution proceeds only by \emph{forking}
        and \emph{completion} moves, never deletion, so that topological
        invariants are conserved by construction;
  \item time is not a dimension of the fundamental structure but an emergent
        bookkeeping of an irreversible solving process;
  \item entropy is attached to frame-invariant causal data, not to spatial
        slices, so that no preferred foliation is required;
  \item the dynamical rule is \emph{derived} from an inference principle
        rather than postulated.
\end{enumerate}

The framework we arrive at is simple to state. The state of the universe is a
finite simplicial set with directed edges. It evolves by two local moves: a
\emph{sprout} attaches a fresh directed edge to a new vertex, creating raw
material; a \emph{fill} completes an unfilled inner horn, attaching a
composite edge together with the higher cell witnessing the composition.
Sprouts create questions; fills answer them. The limit of the filling process
is a quasicategory: a structure in which every composition question has been
answered but directedness --- and with it a native causal orientation --- is
retained. The classical, zero-information limit is the nerve of an ordinary
category, in which all fillers are unique; the physical content of a solved
universe is precisely its distance from nervehood.

Each move is an event. An event depends on exactly those earlier events that
created the cells it consumed, and this dependency relation is a partial
order: the causal order. A reference frame is nothing but a linearization of
this order. The central quantitative object of the paper is the entropy
\[
  S(e) \;=\; \log \#\{\text{linear extensions of } \past{e}\},
\]
the logarithm of the number of ways the causal past of an event could have
been sequenced. This functional is foliation-invariant by construction,
monotone under the dynamics, and measures \emph{causal parallelism}: a fully
sequential past has zero entropy; a fully parallel past of $n$ events has
entropy $\log n!$.

Our contributions are: (i) a precise state space and move set satisfying the
conservation demands above (Section~\ref{sec:framework}); (ii) the
causal-past entropy functional together with statements (and, where complete,
proofs) of its basic properties (Section~\ref{sec:entropy}); (iii) a
formulation of the maximum-entropy inference problem that is intended to
\emph{derive} the dynamical rule (Section~\ref{sec:maxent}); (iv) an argument
that the combinatorial escalation of coherence questions forces decelerating
expansion (Section~\ref{sec:tower}); (v) first numerical results from a toy
implementation (Section~\ref{sec:results}); and (vi) an explicit map of the
design space, recording the alternatives rejected at each decision point so
that they remain available for future exploration
(Section~\ref{sec:designspace}).

We emphasise the register of this paper: it is a \emph{framework} proposal
with a small number of proved statements, one computed experiment, and a
list of hard open problems (Section~\ref{sec:open}), of which the recovery
of the full Lorentz group from causal invariance, and of an area law for the
entropy of causal horizons, are the most severe. Where a proof is incomplete
the text says so, visibly.
