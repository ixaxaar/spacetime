\section{The framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Scope and assumptions}

The starting point is a space of admissible histories, not a growing
spacetime. A history specifies relations between elementary constituents.
It need not come with a preferred sequence in which those constituents
were created. The description is pre-geometric in the limited sense that
no metric, physical dimension, or manifold structure is assigned to it.
Whether it admits a spacetime interpretation is a question to be answered,
not a property conferred by calling it a history.

Three choices have to be kept distinct. Admissibility determines which
histories are allowed. Weighting determines how alternatives contribute to
predictions. A description of records determines which questions those
predictions concern. The framework does not yet derive any of these choices
from the others.

We work at finite size so that the definitions do not presuppose a measure
on an unspecified collection of infinite objects. Removing that restriction
will require a separate limiting construction. No conservation of homotopy
type, growth rule, or global fitness function is imposed. Such conditions
could be added to a particular model, but each would require its own
justification.

\subsection{Admissible histories}
\label{sec:admissibility}

\begin{definition}[Candidate history]
Fix a finite set of labels $L$ and a finite relational signature $\Sigma$,
whose symbols have specified finite arities. A candidate history $H$
consists of a finite nonempty set $E_H$, a label map $\ell_H:E_H\to L$, and
a relation $R_H\subseteq E_H^k$ for every $k$-ary symbol $R\in\Sigma$.
An isomorphism of histories is a bijection of their underlying sets that
preserves labels and all relations.
\end{definition}

The elements of $E_H$ are not assumed to be spacetime points or measurement
events. A directed relation can be included, but its interpretation as
physical causality would require further work. If its transitive closure
is required to be a partial order, acyclicity is an additional constraint.
It does not follow from the definition of a relational structure.

\begin{definition}[Admissibility data]
Admissibility data $\mathcal A$ consist of $L$, $\Sigma$, and a specified
collection of isomorphism-invariant constraints on candidate histories.
For a positive integer $N$, let
\[
  \Omega_N(\mathcal A)
  = \{H : H\text{ satisfies }\mathcal A,\ 1\leq |E_H|\leq N\}/{\cong}.
\]
We use only cutoffs for which this finite set is nonempty.
\end{definition}

The cutoff $N$ limits the size of a description. Increasing it is not
identified with the passage of physical time. The phrase ``all histories''
will always mean all histories admitted by specified data $\mathcal A$,
not all mathematical structures without restriction.

Locality also needs a definition. One available combinatorial notion uses
the incidence graph whose vertices are elements of $E_H$, with two distinct
elements adjacent when they occur together in a relation tuple. A model
can impose conditions on neighbourhoods of a specified finite radius in
this graph. The graph, radius, and allowed patterns are then inputs. This
does not establish locality with respect to a Lorentzian metric. Global
conditions, including acyclicity, must be stated separately rather than
described as consequences of local compatibility without an argument.

Passing to isomorphism classes removes arbitrary names for constituents.
It does not identify distinct histories merely because they share a
topology or some coarse description. Any further identification requires
an explicit equivalence relation and an account of which observations it
preserves.

\gap{Choose a relational signature and admissibility constraints with an
independent structural motivation. Specify the meaning of locality and any
global restrictions. Section~\ref{sec:twopath} specifies a finite test
model, but no choice suitable for a spacetime reconstruction is selected.}

\subsection{Weights and interference}
\label{sec:weights}

Admissibility alone does not supply probabilities. For fixed $N$ and
$\mathcal A$, write $\Omega_N=\Omega_N(\mathcal A)$. A classical model
could assign a probability $p_N(H)$ to each $H\in\Omega_N$. Such a model
adds probabilities for disjoint alternatives and has no interference
between them.

To leave room for interference, we instead introduce the following quantum
weighting. This is an adopted mathematical structure, not a consequence of
the preceding definitions \citep{sorkin1994quantummeasure}.

\begin{definition}[Finite quantum weighting]
A weighting on $\Omega_N$ is a Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix
$K_N$, indexed by pairs of histories, with normalization
\[
  \sum_{H,H'\in\Omega_N} K_N(H,H')=1.
\]
Its decoherence functional on pairs of subsets is
\[
  D_N(A,B)=\sum_{H\in A}\sum_{H'\in B}K_N(H,H').
\]
The positive semidefinite condition on $K_N$ imposes strong positivity.
\end{definition}

The diagonal choice $K_N(H,H')=p_N(H)\delta_{H,H'}$ includes classical
probability as a special case. Off-diagonal entries allow interference.
For disjoint alternatives $A$ and $B$,
\[
  D_N(A\cup B,A\cup B)
  =D_N(A,A)+D_N(B,B)+2\operatorname{Re}D_N(A,B).
\]
Consequently $D_N(A,A)$ is not, in general, an additive probability measure
on all subsets of histories.

The pair $(\mathcal A,K_N)$ supplies finite kinematic and weighting data.
A physical model would also have to distinguish its fixed dynamical law
from preparation or boundary data on which $K_N$ depends. Neither the
admissibility constraints nor this matrix definition selects that law.
Requiring weights to respect a specified local composition of structures
could constrain it, but the composition and the constraint would need to
be supplied explicitly.

\gap{Construct a weighting prescription with a stated physical or
structural justification. Establish its normalization, positivity, and
compatibility with the proposed composition of histories. The complex
weights and strong positivity are currently assumptions, not derived laws.}

\subsection{Alternatives and records}
\label{sec:records}

\begin{definition}[Decoherent family]
A partition $\mathcal P=\{A_1,\ldots,A_m\}$ of $\Omega_N$ is decoherent
when $D_N(A_i,A_j)=0$ for every $i\neq j$. Within that family, assign
\[
  p_N(A_i)=D_N(A_i,A_i).
\]
These numbers are nonnegative and sum to one.
\end{definition}

A partition specifies alternative answers to a question. It does not by
itself describe a measuring device or a physical record. A record model
would need to associate those alternatives with distinguishable structures
inside the histories and explain how their correlations are retained.
Identifying such structures is part of the reconstruction problem.

Different decoherent families need not admit a common decoherent refinement.
The framework therefore does not assume one joint classical probability
distribution for all possible measurement questions. The definition above
uses exact decoherence. Any application using approximate decoherence must
bound the interference terms and the resulting probability errors.

No operators, spectra, or measurement interactions have yet been recovered.
In particular, the existence of this weighting formalism does not derive
the eigenvalues of a quantum observable or the Born rule for a physical
apparatus. Those claims would require a connection between the history
description and a quantum theory of states, operations, and measurements.

\gap{Define internal records and measurement alternatives. Show which
families decohere and with what accuracy. Explain the relation to quantum
observables without assigning independent classical values to all
incompatible measurements.}

\subsection{A finite model of interference and records}
\label{sec:twopath}

The definitions can be tested on a system small enough that every history
and every weight can be written explicitly. The example is a standard
two-path quantum process expressed in the language of the framework.
Its purpose is to expose which information comes from admissibility, which
comes from weighting, and which alternatives can carry probabilities.
It is not a new explanation of interference.

\subsubsection{The four histories}

Take one binary relation and the label set
\[
  L=\{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{route}_0,\mathrm{route}_1,
       \mathrm{out}_0,\mathrm{out}_1\}.
\]
Require a history to contain exactly three vertices, with relation edges
forming a path from an input vertex to one route vertex and then to one
output vertex. No other vertices or relation tuples are allowed. These
are explicit finite boundary and admissibility conditions, not a proposed
universal local law. The four isomorphism classes are
\[
  H_{sd}=(\mathrm{in}\longrightarrow\mathrm{route}_s
                  \longrightarrow\mathrm{out}_d),
  \qquad s,d\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Here $s$ labels a route and $d$ labels an output. Fix $N=3$ and write
$\Omega=\{H_{00},H_{10},H_{01},H_{11}\}$.

All four histories have the same underlying path shape. Their labels
distinguish them. Merely counting them gives no phase dependence and says
nothing about whether the route becomes recorded.

\subsubsection{The composition prescription}

Supply a two-dimensional route space and the mixing matrix
\[
  B=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
  \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The input is the basis state $|0\rangle$. The first operation is $B$,
the route labelled $1$ acquires a phase $e^{i\phi}$, and the last operation
is another $B$. Matrix entries belong to the weighting prescription, not
to the bare relation edges. The phase $\phi$ is a dimensionless setting,
not a clock reading. In the absence of route records the history amplitude
is
\[
  a_{sd}=B_{ds}e^{is\phi}B_{s0}
        =\frac12(-1)^{sd}e^{is\phi}.
\]
The same matrices are used for every setting. We do not fit an independent
weight to each output probability.

To model a possible route record, supply an auxiliary two-dimensional
space and normalized states
\[
  |r_0\rangle=|0\rangle,\qquad
  |r_1\rangle=\gamma|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-\gamma^2}|1\rangle,
  \qquad 0\leq\gamma\leq1.
\]
Between the two mixing operations, route $s$ becomes correlated with
$|r_s\rangle$. Their overlap is $\langle r_0|r_1\rangle=\gamma$.
At $\gamma=1$ the auxiliary state contains no information about the route.
At $\gamma=0$ the two auxiliary states are orthogonal and can distinguish
the routes perfectly. This record space is additional weighting data.
It has not been reconstructed from the three-vertex histories.

With orthogonal output states $|d\rangle$, the branch vector for a history
is
\[
  |\psi_{sd}\rangle=a_{sd}|d\rangle\otimes|r_s\rangle.
\]
The prescribed kernel is
\[
  K(H_{sd},H_{s'd'})
   =\langle\psi_{s'd'}|\psi_{sd}\rangle
   =\delta_{dd'}a_{sd}\overline{a_{s'd'}}
      \langle r_{s'}|r_s\rangle.
\]
In the stated ordering of $\Omega$ this is
\begin{equation}
  K=\frac14
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1&\gamma e^{-i\phi}&0&0\\
    \gamma e^{i\phi}&1&0&0\\
    0&0&1&-\gamma e^{-i\phi}\\
    0&0&-\gamma e^{i\phi}&1
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:toykernel}
\end{equation}

This specifies a family of finite weightings on one unchanged possibility
space. The matrix $B$, complex amplitudes, tensor-product record space, and
inner-product prescription are quantum assumptions. Locality here means
factorization across the stated operations. It is not locality in an
emergent spacetime. The input preparation and the choice of operation order
are also supplied, rather than derived.

\subsubsection{The questions asked of the model}

The output alternatives are
\[
  A_d=\{H_{0d},H_{1d}\},
\]
and the route alternatives are
\[
  C_s=\{H_{s0},H_{s1}\}.
\]
The output question forgets the route. The joint route-and-output question
retains every history separately. A distinction between these partitions
is therefore a concrete test of whether discarding microscopic labels is
legitimate classical coarse-graining or requires interference terms.

Section~\ref{sec:toyresults} gives the resulting probabilities and their
dependence on record overlap. The calculation has no statistical sampling
error because it uses four histories exactly. No laboratory comparison or
simulation is reported.

\subsection{Time and invariance}
\label{sec:time}

The weighted space of histories is specified without a process that chooses
one history after another. This removes the need for an external clock
governing such a process. It does not establish that the physical universe
is timeless, nor does it explain the occurrence of a definite measurement
outcome.

A candidate internal clock would be a structure whose distinguishable
readings correlate with other records. If the joint alternatives for a
clock reading $t$ and another record $r$ form a decoherent partition, their
conditional probabilities can be written
\[
  p_N(r\mid t)
  =\frac{D_N(A_r\cap A_t,A_r\cap A_t)}{D_N(A_t,A_t)},
  \qquad D_N(A_t,A_t)>0.
\]
This is a way to express relational predictions, not a derivation of
dynamics \citep{page1983}. A useful clock also requires ordered readings,
appropriate correlations, and a regime in which it reliably parametrizes
other changes. Conditional probabilities alone provide none of these.

An arrow of time would require an asymmetry in the relevant record
correlations or boundary conditions. It is not supplied by the order in
which a computer enumerates a history. Distinct microscopic histories can
agree on clock readings, but that fact does not establish a second hidden
time or a physical process outside the clock description.

Likewise, a fixed space of possibilities is not a conservation law.
Relabelling invariance is built into $\Omega_N$. Independence of predictions
from a cutoff, a presentation, or an added equivalence relation is a
different requirement. Each must specify the transformation and the
predictions claimed to remain unchanged. Homotopy equivalence alone does
not imply equality of geometry or physical observations.

\gap{Construct an internal clock and identify its domain of validity.
Determine which record correlations distinguish a temporal direction.
No physical time observable or arrow of time has been derived.}

\subsection{Effective geometry and matter}
\label{sec:geometry}

A spacetime reconstruction would assign geometric descriptions to suitable
coarse alternatives, not identify every microscopic constituent with a
spacetime point. It must specify what is being coarse-grained, which
histories contribute to an alternative, and when a metric description is
accurate. If a causal order is used, its origin and its relation to
operational causality must be stated. A volume measure and physical scale
cannot be inferred from the word ``order.''

The target is a regime in which effective geometry and matter predictions
come from the same admissibility and weighting data. A Lorentzian metric
without gravitational dynamics would not recover general relativity.
Random outcomes without interference, the appropriate observable algebra,
and quantum correlations would not recover quantum field theory. Fitting
these two descriptions independently would not establish their common
origin.

Cosmological expansion would have to be expressed through an effective
metric relative to internal clock readings. Neither an increase in the
cutoff $N$ nor a greater number of admissible histories is, by definition,
an increase in spatial volume. No general principle requiring a closed
system to settle into its lowest-energy configuration is assumed.

Entropy also depends on the question being asked. For a decoherent family,
$-\sum_i p_N(A_i)\log p_N(A_i)$ is a Shannon entropy of those alternatives.
It is not automatically thermodynamic entropy, entanglement entropy, or
horizon entropy. Counting microscopic descriptions or their enumerations
does not establish an area law.

\gap{O1. Recover an effective Lorentzian geometry and its local Lorentz
symmetry. Identify a regime in which gravitational and matter predictions
recover general relativity and quantum field theory from the same model.}

\subsection{Robustness of effective laws}
\label{sec:robustness}

The proposed explanation of effective laws is that they might persist
across a range of microscopic choices. This is a question about a family
of models, not a property established by giving one model a complicated
state space. It requires specified changes to admissibility or weighting
and a way to compare the same coarse questions across those changes.

For example, suppose two models give probability distributions $p_i$ and
$\widetilde p_i$ on matched coarse alternatives, each in a decoherent
family. An operational tolerance can be expressed as
\[
  \frac12\sum_i |p_i-\widetilde p_i|\leq\epsilon.
\]
A robustness claim must name the alternatives, the permitted microscopic
changes, the tolerance, and the regime over which the bound is meant to
hold. This comparison does not require an external physical time.

Persistence within a history is a separate property. It requires a way to
recognize related records at different internal clock readings. Neither
form of stability implies that a configuration is probable. In the
interfering model, probability claims must refer to alternatives in a
decoherent family rather than to an assumed classical distribution over
all individual histories.

No global fitness function is needed for these questions. Conversely,
neither local compatibility nor stability alone is claimed to force the
observed laws. A model would have to exhibit the proposed regime and show
which microscopic details its predictions do not depend on.

\gap{Specify a family of microscopic models, matched coarse records, and
allowed perturbations. Establish a nontrivial regime of robust predictions
and distinguish this from persistence under an internal clock. No such
regime has yet been demonstrated.}

\subsection{Inference and limits}
\label{sec:inference}

An inference principle could restrict the remaining choices, but its prior
and constraints are part of the model. On the classical diagonal subclass,
one possible finite problem is
\[
  \underset{p_N\in\mathcal F_N}{\operatorname{maximize}}
  \left[-\sum_{H\in\Omega_N}p_N(H)
  \log\frac{p_N(H)}{\mu_N(H)}\right],
\]
where $\mu_N$ is a specified strictly positive reference probability and
$\mathcal F_N$ is a specified feasible set of probability distributions.
Terms with $p_N(H)=0$ are taken to be zero. This is a relative-entropy
inference problem \citep{jaynes1957}, not a derivation of the prior or the
constraints. A uniform distribution over isomorphism classes is itself a
choice and need not agree with counting labelled descriptions.

This classical problem does not determine off-diagonal quantum weights.
Any inference principle for $K_N$ must state its objective, admissible
variations, and justification independently. No Gibbs form or unique
quantum law is asserted here.

Finally, the family of finite models is not yet a theory of an infinite
universe. A limiting proposal must specify how coarse records at different
cutoffs correspond and whether their probabilities converge. It must also
control dependence on the chosen regulator. Simply restricting a matrix
from $\Omega_{N+1}$ to $\Omega_N$ need not preserve its normalization.
